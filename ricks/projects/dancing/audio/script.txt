In fifteen eighteen, a woman started dancing in the street, and could not stop. Her name, the chronicles say, was Frau Troffea. It was July, in the city of Strasbourg. She danced for days. Within a week, more than thirty people had joined her. None of them wanted to. By August, some chronicles count four hundred. The city council asked the physicians. Their verdict: overheated blood. The cure? More dancing. So the council opened the guild halls, and hired musicians to keep everyone moving. It only made things worse. You see, nobody could dance the fever out, because it was never in their blood. Historians think the city was worn down by famine and disease, and people believed an angry saint could curse them to dance. Fear did the rest. So the council banned the music, and sent the dancers up a mountain, to the shrine of Saint Vitus, in red shoes. And there, the dancing stopped. The cure for a dancing plague was never more dancing. It was believing you were forgiven.
